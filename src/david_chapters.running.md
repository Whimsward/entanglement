# Running

| David |
| :---: |

In many places, the Kalamazoo River is surrounded by ravine that was carved back when the river was a thing elemental in nature and not just an involuntary repository for the waste products of human civilization. If you don't look at the river directly it can be stunning at times, a snapshot of the river dragging generations of trees into its murk.

David picks his way along a deer path, several steps ahead of Lila. “The first thing they did when I showed up was try to kill me,” he says. “They thought I was him, playing a trick.”

“I tried to kill him first, too,” Lila says. “He's the kind of bad you don't play games with.”

There is a raspberry bush half-buried by leaves. David bends and plucks a berry. It tastes just fine. “They said he took over their country.”

“That's probably his strategy for ‘fixing things.’”

“Something to watch out for,” he says. “when we’re not scrambling to keep him from killing everyone we love.”

“He won’t target our parents.”

“But we’re in this, and that means they won’t stay out of it.”

“How long do you think we have?”

“It’s been, what, an hour?” David pushes through a pricklebush. He only notices because a barb catches in his shirt. Lila brushes past it, and the plant withers. “So any second now, probably.”

“And we’re just going to hide until then?”

David tromps. “I am making distance between Thrast and anywhere important, because, and this is only if I actually understand what’s happening, he’ll come back wherever I am. Making distance is the only thing I can do to him, short of maybe locking myself in a faraday cage.”

“This is not electromagnetic trickery,” Lila assures him, with words put together in binary and parsed back into English. The emergency alert system overrides her preferences and delivers an amber alert. “This is metaphysical hacking.”

“So the people I met on the other side were talking about alignments and paradigms,” David says. “They said I should be ‘aligned with all five.’”

“There must be five.”

“The way you said that’s not at all disturbing.”

“You looking for answers or just chatting?”

“I don’t know. Looking for a diplomatic way to repeat ‘what the hell did you do to yourself?’”

“I divided my being into five parts.”

“But WHY?” He turns, his whole head flushing hot.

Carefully, she tells her brother the truth. “It was a ring in a puddle in a cave, and I won the riddle.”

“I am not gonna have the argument right now about what if Boromir found the ring instead of Bilbo.”

“Arwen.”

“Galadriel? Wait. Stop. Listen. What is your actual vision, Li?”

“I'm going to figure it out.”

“'It’ is?”

“Everything?”

David looks around him. “Okay. Okay.”

Silence falls over wild places when humans intrude, but the stillness now is deeper than that. Lila is out of place, with an arm made of yarn and a ring of fire on her hand. Part of her is questing through the woods, and David can see now the vine here and bush there that have crumbled into ashes. He doesn't challenge her on that. There isn't time. They're maybe two miles from the road. If he could've snagged a car they might have gotten farther from home, but this is where they got.

“Okay,” he says. “We should split up. If he can't find you, maybe you can figure yourself out, do some good.” He doesn't voice his plea that she's planning to do *good*. “Hide, if you can? I'm gonna go as nuts as I can to screw him up.”

He turns toward the river.

Before he can go, Lila asks, “David? Are we good?”

“We're alive, aren't we?”

At first his descent is careful, despite himself. When you spend seventeen years of your life learning how to steward your body, you don’t unlearn that care quickly--unless something traumatic shears off your need for self-preservation. But David reminds himself he could only have seconds, and maybe momentum is conserved when you blink across realities, so he picks up his pace and summarily loses his balance. He keels forward. His head cracks against a root but he feels no pain. He doesn’t see the root splinter because he is too busy completing the first of a dozen or so involuntary somersaults. With every impact he anticipates a complaint from his nerves that never comes. It is a painless, loud, clumsy descent from the slope into the river.

It’s the Kalamazoo river in mid-August. It hasn’t rained for about two weeks. Here, a couple miles from his parents’ house, the river runs lazy. He is not hurried off to some other part of the river. But David is dragged under by the current, and it pushes him down. Down towards the riverbed. Downstream. Down, into muck that obscures the sun even from an arm’s length beneath the surface.

His eyes are wide open, and dirt and the water don’t so much as sting them. He can faintly feel the place where an invisible barrier stops water from running into his ears or nose. He waits for his lungs to start burning, but they never do.

There, in the murk that drags him along, he feels no physical discomfort. But the dark starts to look a little bit like nothing. And maybe, in the midst of that nothing, he can glimpse dull gray stonework stretching out to either end of oblivion.
